Brief prompt · Powerhouse Museum collectionHow can a visitor’s understanding, interpretation or appreciation of this object be enhanced or enlivened through interaction with digital technologies?

Brief

A university assignment: design an interactive digital experience for one object. I chose Christopher Dresser’s cast-iron Garden Chair, 1870.

Insight

We see the chair as either a utilitarian object or a piece of art, a division that reflects design for standardisation rather than an intrinsic truth.

Response

An immersive installation inspired by his philosophy that true appreciation of beauty arises from contextual knowledge and understanding.

01Research · Reading Dresser · Aug

Pleasure is derived from that which is understood.

Christopher Dresser, The Art of Decorative Design, 1862

Tracing the chair, reading the books

Once I started reading Dresser’s publications, I pulled out the figures he references in each chapter and made an annotated mind map of these, which allowed me to visually see the connections between ideas and concepts.

I read The Art of Decorative Design (1862) and The Principles of Decorative Design (1873) entirely through.

My first mind map of the chair’s context kept growing and needed constant reorganising.

Redrawing his ornament, motif by motif
Key ideaIf visitors learn and appreciate Dresser’s principles on the way, they will recognise the same beauty when they see the chair.
How might weReimagine Dresser’s ornamental imagery to engage and educate the contemporary audience on the intersections between science, nature and the arts?

03Concept 02 · Projection · Sep

From headset to room

A headset keeps the experience to one person, and studio feedback was to focus on the experience, not the technology.

I moved to projection mapping inside sheer walls, with light projected onto fabric so groups explore together.

03Visual narrative · 21 Sep

Visitors can enter the installation from multiple pathways, where light projections transform the walls and floor into dynamic canvases.

The illustrations evolve as viewers move through the exhibition, immersing and engaging them as active participants.

A botanical form generates on the floor under each person’s footstep, and the projections intensify upon approaching the chair.

Interactive symbols that encircle the seat illuminate a specific aspect of the design.

04Prototype 1 · Spatial navigation · 10 Oct

Paths at small scale

A small-scale model to test the intuitiveness of navigating various pathways, and whether visitors felt encouraged to explore freely.

Material exploration

Paper

Dropped
Pros
Easy to manipulate, readily available.
Cons
Lacked structural integrity, tore easily, unsuitable for interaction.

Fabric

Dropped
Pros
Semi-translucent, good light reflection resembling the intended final look.
Cons
Required excessive reinforcement, which obstructed pathways.

Gutter guard

Kept
Pros
Malleable, durable, easy to join securely without adhesives.
Cons
Sharp edges, so users moved a Blu Tack figurine on a flexible wire instead of touching the mesh.
Original aim

Assess purely navigational aspects.

Revised aim

Observe user engagement with pathways, including entry and exit choices and recognition of the central area’s importance.

05Prototype 2 · Interaction · 15 Oct

Show, don’t tell

Stepping on a floor symbol played the geometric construction of one of Dresser’s ornaments, with no instruction or prior knowledge.

Try it · step on the symbol
Idle · pulse or glowActive · line to projection
Observed

One user tried to sit on the symbol and two reached to touch it.

Decided

Orientation is the only requirement: face the chair.

06Prototype 3 · Immersion · 24 Oct

Wizard of Oz

I manually adjusted the projector in relation to the users’ movements while remaining out of sight, with natural ambient sounds and no instructions.

“Made the space feel more alive.”

Movement

Users moved slowly along the curve of the wall, and when they paused, they recognised they had influence over the visuals.

Meaning

All users understood that by the end of the path, the visuals were communicating the geometry behind the final shape.

Testers asked for subtle ambient elements, like grass and plants flowing as they walk past. Manual tracking lagged at times, which automated motion tracking would resolve.

Two prototypes, read together
Prototype 1

Curves slow people down.

Prototype 3

Pausing is when people notice they move the projections.

Decided

More curved routes at the entry points, so visitors understand their role straight away.

07Final experience · 1 Nov

Enter, path, centre, chair

Steps placed on prototype 1’s model

The outcome is the realisation that this seemingly conventional object encompasses all of the concepts examined.

08Reflection

Basic findings from seemingly independent prototypes, when combined, result in a significantly more comprehensive and effective design solution.

Prototype 1 helped me appreciate the advantages of failure. The material complications redirected my focus towards analysing user interactions more attentively.
